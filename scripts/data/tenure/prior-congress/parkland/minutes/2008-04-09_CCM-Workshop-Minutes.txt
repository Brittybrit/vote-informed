busy with foreclosures and lien activity through the Special Master proceedings, and he outlined

the specific actions required by subsection (a) of Resolution No. 2004-32 with respect to fines 
and mitigation. 
Mayor Udine recalled that the Commission had considered a mitigation at its last meeting. He 
believed that the process of holding a request for mitigation until the last Wednesday of the 
month for the Special Master hearing and then a subsequent Commission meeting was unwieldy 
as, in the interim, deals may be lost. He suggested that, at least on a temporary basis, the City 
Attorney should be able to work with banks and real estate agents, or potential buyers and their 
attorneys, particularly in the case of short sales, to try to close the transactions and get the houses 
again occupied. 
City Attorney Andrew Maurodis suggested preparation of a resolution to establish a truncated 
procedure for handling by him and the staff. The resolution would include some standards, such 
as for compliance, and he asked the Commission’s recommendation of other guidelines. He 
noted that these matters would then not come to the Commission, except in the case of an appeal. 
Mayor Udine believed that the property must be brought into compliance and that the City 
should at least recoup its costs. 
Commissioner Rosenof offered that the Planning and Zoning Board might be considered as 
another avenue. He believed that the City’s costs should be the absolute bottom line, and that 
both a short sale and an equity sale – even a $100 equity sale -- should be fairly treated. 
Mayor Udine suggested that, while he had used the short sale as an example, an owner walking 
away with equity should have brought the property into compliance. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz agreed with the points of both the Mayor and Commissioner Rosenof, 
but he believed the City Attorney should be given the flexibility of when to apply the rule. 
Mayor Udine reiterated that he was not in favor if the owner who caused the violation walked 
away with equity, and he did not want the City to impede the sale. In the case of a $100 equity 
sale and a much greater lien amount, he suggested that the City Attorney should be able to 
reduce the lien, but the lien would become the cost amount plus the $100. He did not want to 
preclude those individuals from being able to sell their homes. 
The Mayor commented that there would be no advantage to the use of the Planning and Zoning 
Board since they met just once a month. He would rather have the City Attorney handle it, and 
he suggested that the resolution could include the provision that the City Attorney would notify 
each Commissioner of proposals and give them 24 or 48 hours to object. 
Mr. Maurodis believed this a good point, and he indicated that he and the City Manager would 
prepare the resolution for the next meeting. Mr. Maurodis offered information about Fort 
Lauderdale’s “call-up” method.

Mayor Udine described a scenario in which time was of the essence, with the City Attorney 
advising the Commission one-on-one of the circumstances. He suggested that, with a 
Commission objection, the matter would then be placed on an agenda. The Mayor believed the 
City’s position should be to assist with the sales. 
Commissioner Rosenof agreed with the Mayor, but struggled with the arbitrariness of long sale 
versus short sale; he suggested that some individuals would get special privileges for losing 
money. He would prefer that the City Attorney have authority to handle all liens. 
Mr. Maurodis suggested that the process could include the completion of a form providing 
information. The appeal process would be important, but staff would make its best judgment. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz understood that the City should not be an impediment to property sales; 
however, the City should recoup its costs and help facilitate the process. He believed 
Commissioner Rosenof’s was the correct argument. 
Mayor Udine noted that anyone could ask for this assistance, but the City Attorney will 
understand that the request would not be approved if the owner was walking away with funds. 
Mr. Maurodis commented that it would be a function between the City Manager and City 
Attorney. Staff input would be vital as to its costs. He noted current discussions about a 
property in violation for a year, and Mayor Udine commented that it would not be approved 
because it had not met compliance – with which Mr. Maurodis agreed. 
Mayor Udine offered that anyone could ask, but it should be the policy of the Commission that 
the request would not be approved if the owner was walking away with dollars. 
At the Vice Mayor’s request for clarification about those turned down, Mayor Udine responded 
there would be the normal appeal process through the City Commission. The City Attorney’s 
handling would be an expedited way of handling them. 
Commissioner Rosenof questioned how often the City has been confronted with these situations, 
and Ms. Gardner-Young stated in the past month there had been two. It was becoming more 
common, and Mayor Udine agreed that it was becoming a larger issue. 
With discussion about the handling of foreclosures by homeowners’ associations, Vice Mayor 
Moskowitz suggested getting in touch with them to get the status of what was happening within 
their communities. 
Mayor Udine agreed, but felt that not a lot of staff time should be spent because many, many 
properties are affected. He noted that many assessments at Heron Bay were late and that 
numbers were steadily increasing. The associations were filing liens, but not foreclosing since 
their liens were so far down in priority that it may not make sense. 
Mayor Udine summarized there was Commission consensus to proceed as discussed.

Mr. Archer explained the foreclosure procedure. Once the lien has been filed and not paid, the 
Code provides that the Special Master can authorize a foreclosure action after three months, 
which is then presented to the City Commission. He outlined the four foreclosure options. 
Mayor Udine commented that he and the City Manager had spoken about unpaid liens on vacant 
properties, which he believed warranted consideration of a foreclosure action by the City. The 
City could recover its lien amounts, along with its costs and fees. He suggested that the City 
should follow subsection (b) of the Resolution, and the City Manager could bring several of 
these under one future agenda item. Notice to the property owner would have been served, and 
the Commission can decide whether the City Attorney should proceed with foreclosure. 
Ms. Gardner-Young reviewed her past experience with foreclosure actions by other 
municipalities. She anticipated several cases for the next meetings, and she felt that this City 
action belonged within the purview of the City Commission. 
City Attorney Maurodis offered that the Code may have to be changed through an amendment to 
the ordinance. He believed that the most common occurrence in the future would be that the 
City would be a defendant in foreclosure actions. Mayor Udine suggested filing Notice of 
Appearance or Affirmative Defense and have no further involvement, and Mr. Maurodis agreed. 
 1. DISCUSSION: PINE TREE SWALES 
City Manager Caryn Gardner-Young recalled that this subject had been discussed previously 
with the Commission and a number of options were presented for consideration. Staff had been 
given direction to examine two options: code enforce non-compliant properties or imposition of 
a special assessment through placement on the property tax bill. A direct billing of the resident 
had not been examined. 
Public Works Director Jim Berkman stated that staff had examined the costs of code 
enforcement as opposed to the City taking over maintenance of the swales. Information as to the 
costs were compiled by BSO and the Finance, Environmental Resources, and Public Works 
Departments and provided in the backup. He noted that a recent BSO operation had shown that 
only 72 of 272 homes in that area were in compliance, which equated to a 76 percent non- 
compliance rate. Staff looked to the Commission for direction. 
Ms. Gardner-Young suggested that, to continue on the code enforcement process, the part-time 
code enforcement position must be upgraded to a full-time position, plus a dedicated Gator to 
maneuver in that area. From the code enforcement aspect, she estimated a minimum of $50,000 
for code enforcement, and she suggested that it would take over a year to get through the entire 
Pinetree area and remain ongoing. As to the special assessment process, she spoke regarding the 
costs for implementation, which would not include the costs for bringing the swales into 
condition and the maintenance. She stated that, for approximately $9,000, the swales could be 
assessed by an engineer.

Vice Mayor Moskowitz questioned the cost of $9,000 versus the cost in excess of $25,000 
included in the package. Mr. Berkman noted that, should the City take over maintenance, the 
NPDES would require that capital projects also be undertaken. 
The Vice Mayor recapped what the total costs would be for the two different options, and Mr. 
Berkman provided further clarification. 
For the record, Mayor Udine noted that there had been no suggestion that it would be the City’s 
responsibility with City dollars. He recalled that their last discussion had been to follow the 
code enforcement method; in reality, the City would bear some costs with Option 1, but the 
people causing the problem must take care of it. Ms. Gardner-Young added that, should the 
property owner not bring the property into compliance, there would be costs to the City to do the 
work. With homesteaded property, it could not be liened, and City Attorney Andrew Maurodis 
agreed there would be a high non-payment rate. Ms. Gardner-Young suggested that funds must 
be budgeted each year in anticipation of these costs. 
Commissioner Rosenof commented that he questioned the 76 percent non-compliance. While he 
acknowledged that he had issues with property owners downstream from his property, he had 
maintained his swale. He felt it unfair that he should be assessed because neighbors had not 
done what they should; the people who cause the problem should be the ones charged. Either 
solution would be better than what currently exists, but he preferred the code enforcement 
option. 
Mayor Udine agreed with Commissioner Rosenof. He felt that the code enforcement option 
should be followed for at least the first year. 
There was some discussion concerning purchase of the Gator. Captain O’Connell commented 
that two deputies sent out in the City’s Gator had been badly scratched. They wanted a vehicle 
with some protective enclosure for the officers’ safety. 
Mayor Udine reiterated his belief that Option 1 would be the proper way to start this process. 
His only fear was that Option 1 would not reach its ultimate goal and that people may have to be 
constantly cited. He felt the City should give Option 1 a chance to work; Option 2 may have to 
be placed in effect next year. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz suggested that a letter should be sent to property owners about the 
prospective code enforcement action. Ms. Gardner-Young indicated that letters had already been 
sent, informing property owners that it was their responsibility to maintain the swale area behind 
their homes. She noted that property owners had already been cited, and it was felt that another 
letter was unnecessary. 
Captain O’Connell added that, in what he described as Phase 1 of their operation, 76 percent of 
the properties were in non-compliance. Deputies would commence Phase 2, east of Parkside 
Drive and south of Holmberg Road, next week and then Phase 3, east of Parkside and north of 
Holmberg. He noted that there may be different compliance.

 3. DISCUSSION: HOLMBERG ROAD LANDSCAPE THEME 
 DESIGN CONCEPTS 
Environmental Services Director Brian Archer stated that recent buffer removals have influenced 
the amendment of the Holmberg Road Landscape Theme to require that removed buffers be 
replaced within 90 days and achieve opaqueness within one year. With lot clearing, recent 
regulations require that all invasives be removed. He outlined the proposed options: maintain 
the Holmberg Road Landscape Theme as drafted; maintain the Holmberg Road Landscape 
Theme but require that all invasives be replaced with a different type of buffer; a landscape plan, 
site specific, would be approved by the City for each parcel abutting Holmberg Road; and the 
development by the City of several typical landscape plans, with the property owner able to 
choose from those plans for their property. 
Commissioner Smith indicated that he would prefer the option that the City develop several 
different plans, rather than having individual homeowners come before the Commission. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz agreed. He recalled previous discussions and had considered the 
definition of opaque buffer as vague. He did not believe Holmberg Road should be master 
planned. He expressed concern about the properties already landscaped and felt that “opaque”

should be clearly defined. The Vice Mayor cautioned that many residents liked the look and feel 
of Holmberg Road, reminiscent of old Parkland; for better or worse, the invasives were part of it, 
and he did not want it to become an east versus west issue. He believed there should be a 
balance between being environmentally friendly and taking steps to remove invasives. 
Commissioner Rosenof commented there was a fine line between a master plan and criteria. He 
disagreed with Commissioner Smith, preferring to have the creativity of landscape architects. 
He suggested there may be a recognized definition of opacity. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz added that Brazilian pepper was truly opaque as it strangled everything 
else out. He spoke regarding the high cost of being environmental and stated his position, for 
example, that Cypresshead should not be made to remove Brazilian pepper when homeowners 
were recently assessed for the new entrance. He believed that the problem would eventually fix 
itself. While some guidance may be offered, he believed the City should take a minimal role. 
Commissioner Rosenof offered that, while he considered himself an advocate for old Parkland, 
he would rather have it look like it was not abandoned property. He would favor removal of the 
Brazilian pepper by Cypresshead. 
Mayor Udine agreed that much of this would become a financial issue, and he believed it should 
be left along its current lines. As plans were presented, something similar to what had been done 
for the Girl Scouts could occur. 
Vice Mayor Moskowitz felt that some direction from the City should be given, and he asked Mr. 
Archer’s explanation of an opaque buffer. 
Mr. Archer responded that, while it was not specifically defined in the Code, the Code did give 
requirements, such as so many trees, accents, shrubs, and berm per 100-foot section. It was 
designed to provide a top-to-bottom buffer by these different layers. 
Mayor Udine recalled that the Commission had attempted to form definitions in the past; he felt 
that many decision must be made “on the fly” as it becomes before the Planning and Zoning 
Board, the Community Appearance Board, and the Commission. He believed there no perfect 
definitions. 
City Attorney Andrew Maurodis believed the Code contained fairly good detail. 
Ms. Gardner-Young commented that not every property along Holmberg Road would come 
before the Commission. She noted that the Girl Scouts had needed site plan approval; single- 
family homes would not. 
Mayor Udine offered that single-family homes would have to apply for their building permit and 
a landscaping permit, with requirements defined in the Code. Mr. Archer noted that the City’s 
new Ordinance dealing with lot clearing would no longer allow Brazilian pepper to remain as a 
buffer.
