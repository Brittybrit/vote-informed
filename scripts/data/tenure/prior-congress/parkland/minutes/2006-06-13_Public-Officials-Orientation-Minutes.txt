2. SUNSHINE LAW

Mr. Andrew Maurodis, City Attorney, stated that all members of a board are governed by the
Sunshine Law, which allows the public to see the workings of their government. All meetings of
any board or commission of any state agency must be a formal meeting at which there are notes
taken. In addition, the meetings need to be noticed, not necessarily in the newspaper, but the
time, date and location of the meeting must be posted. An agenda is not required for a public
meeting, but if they have one, they can add items to it during the meeting, except if it is a
statutory meeting. Meetings are not required to be recorded, however minutes must be taken;
they can be very general as long as the votes of each person are noted. Every member of a board
must vote unless there is a conflict of interest.

Mr. Maurodis continued to explain that anything that could possibly be the subject of an action of
that board is not allowed to be discussed between board members unless it is at an official
meeting. This includes a discussion by phone, notes or email. Advisory boards are also governed
by these laws. A board may form a subcommittee of the board to perform a specific task, and if
that subcommittee is purely fact finding, they are not governed by the Sunshine Law. However, if
the purpose of the board is to make a recommendation, then they are governed. Mr. Maurodis
advised the board members to take a cautious approach and consider subcommittees as governed
by the Sunshine Law. Inspection trips are exempt from the Sunshine Law if they don't talk about
the subject; the discussion must take place at a noticed meeting. In addition, the board must make
it reasonably possible for citizens to attend meetings. Two members of the same board can attend
a meeting of a separate entity and even offer their opinion to a group. Two members of different
boards can talk to each other. All documents which are related to board membership are
considered public record, including email.Itis permissible for board members to write to each

5. OVERVIEW: ROBERT RULES OF ORDER

Mr. Maurodis, City Attorney, stated that motions are divided into four classes: main motions, in
which all business is conducted, subsidiary motions, incidental motions and frivolous motions.

All motions are superior to a main motion. If someone makes a motion after a general motion is
made, then you vote on the second motion first. Once a motion is made, it is owned by the
assembly, not an individual. There is no debate on a motion to table. If a discussion is
contentious, someone can make a motion to call the question and force the vote. Motion to
postpone indefinitely allows for a debate. A motion to reconsider is done after the main motion
has been passed. They can make this motion at the same meeting or at the next consecutive
meeting. It renders the original vote annulled. The motion most be made by someone on the
prevailing side of the original motion. They are considered to be on the prevailing side if you
were absent during the original vote on the motion. There are no restrictions as to who can
second the motion.

1. The act refers to "any state agency or authority. or any agency or authority of any county.

The statute should be construed so as to frustrate all evasive devices.

Gradison. id.). Application to purely advisory boards is not so clear. In Cape Publications. Inc. v.
The City of Palm Bay. et aI., 473 So.2d 222 (Fla. 5 DCA 1985), it was held that a committee of
individuals who joined the city manager to ask technical questions of applicants for chief of police
was not subject to the Sunshine Law. However, some cases find just the opposite. In Krause v.
Reno, 366 So.2d 1244 (Fla. 1979), the Supreme Court of Florida held that an advisory board of
citizens reporting to the city manager on a selection of a new chief of police was subject to the
Sunshine Law. In News-Press Publishing Company. Inc. v. Carlson, 410 So.2d 546 (Fla. 2d DCA
1982), the Sunshine Law was applied to a committee composed of staff which was responsible for
preparing a budget for a hospital district. The committee in this case had no power to bind the
hospital district but was merely submitting a budget for the district board's approval. The court held
that the committee played an integral part in the decision making process and therefore, its meetings
should be open to the public. The Attorney General has also ruled that an ad hoc committee
appointed by the mayor for purposes of making recommendations concerning legislation was subject
to the Sunshine Law. (AGO 85-76). In a recent ruling it was held that a committee (of staff members
and one outside person) created to assist a purchasing director and advise her in evaluating contract
proposals was governed by the Sunshine Law. The court noted that the committee ranked proposals.
Silver Express Company v. Miami Dade Community College. 691 So.2d 1099 (Fla. 3rd DCA 1997).
The key to this case was the "structured recommendations" emanating from the committee's actions
and the formality of their role. The prudent approach is to consider any board or committee
specifically and formally created to make detailed recommendations to a public agency, subject to
the Sunshine Law - even if the recommendations are non-binding.
There is a distinction between merely fact finding committees and advisory committees. If
the function is more in the line of "fact finding" there is no violation. In Bennett v. Warden, 333
So.2d 97 (Fla. 2d DCA 1976), it was held that a committee appointed by a university president to
report to him on employee working conditions was not subject to the Sunshine Law. The fact
finding concept often gets a broad application. For instance, the Attorney General has held that
where a board member who was authorized to explore various contract proposals with a prospective

Where a government agency formally delegates its authority to a single individual (or group
of staff members), there is a possibility that the actions of that single individual could be considered
as constituting the actions of the agency itself. Therefore, the Sunshine Law could apply to action
of a single individual under certain circumstances. (AGO 74-294). Again, the fact finding
distinction has been made; where an individual has been authorized only to gather information, the
Sunshine Law would not apply. Florida STOP. Inc. v. Goodrum, Case No. 80-3775 (10 Cir. Polk
County, 1980). One of the key factors is whether there has been a formal delegation of authority.
Additionally, the formal nature of the proceedings or recommendations of any group of staff
members will be considered. Finally, where the person acting has authority to act by ordinance,
charter, or law with the authority to take action, there is no Sunshine Law issue.
The issue of delegation is one about which governments must constantly be cognizant. A
formally constituted _negotiating committee would be subject to the Sunshine Law even ifthe final
decision rests with the city commission.
The Sunshine Law is applicable to meetings between a board member and an individual who
is not a member ofthe board when that individual is being used as a liaison between, or to conduct
a de facto meeting of, board members. See, AGO 74-47 (city manager is not a member of the city
council and thus, may meet with individual council members; however, the manager may not act as
a liaison for board members by circulating information and thoughts of individual council members).
Compare, AGO 89-39 (aides to county commissioners would not be subject to the Sunshine Law
unless they have been formally delegated decision-making functions outside of the ambit of normal
staff functions, are acting as liaisons between board members, or are acting in place of the board or
its members at their direction).
For example, in Blackford v. School Board of Orange County, 375 So. 2d 578 (Fla. 5th DCA
1979), the court held that a series of scheduled successive meetings between the school
superintendent and individual members of the school board were subject to the Sunshine Law.
While normally meetings between the school superintendent and an individual school board member
would not be subject to s. 286.011, F.S., these meetings were held in "rapid-fire succession" in order

4. Luncheon Meetings/Out of Town Meetings: The Attorney General's Office frowns upon
luncheon meetings at which public business is conducted. The reasoning is that this may discourage
members of the public from attending or participating. This would be applicable to meetings at
restaurants where the public is more likely to be excluded or limited. The Attorney General's Office
is particularly concerned with luncheon meetings at which the discussion of the board or committee
members is audible only to those at the table. This does not prohibit social gatherings. Further, it
does not prohibit inspection trips, though the Attorney General has also cautioned that this type of
trip must be carefully conducted and has warned that they are not the most desirable way to conduct
business. (AGO 071-361).
The courts have recognized that the mere fact that a meeting is held in a public room does
not make it public within the meaning ofthe Sunshine Law. Bigelow v. Howze, 291 So. 2d 645, 647-
648 (Fla. 2d DCA 1974). For a meeting to be "public," the public must b~ given advance notice and
provided with a reasonable opportunity to attend. [d.
Accordingly, a school board workshop held outside county limits over 100 miles away from
the board's headquarters violated the Sunshine Law where the only advantages to the board resulting
from the out-of-town gathering (elimination of travel time and expense due to the fact that the board

having a reasonable opportunity to attend. Rhea v. School Board of Alachua County, 636 So. 2d
1383 (Fla. pt DCA 1994). As stated by the court, "[t]he interests of the public in having a

Thus, generally, in such forms of government, discussions between a single commissioner and the
mayor need not be held in the Sunshine. The key element In making the decision as to whether such
an item under this form of government is governed by the Sunshine Law is whether the matter is one
which must be discussed or decided at a commission meeting.
8. Private Organizations: Certain private org~zations may be governed by the Sunshine
Law based upon the relationship with the public agency. If there has been a specific delegation of
governmental authority or function to the private agency then, in that event, it will be subject to the
Sunshine Law. A private organization may not be used as a vehicle for circumvention of the
Sunshine Law. (AGO 97-27). Where a private organization acts on behalf of a governmental body
it may be considered a "surrogate public body," thus governed by the Sunshine Law. News Journal
v. Memorial Hospital, 695 So.2d 418 (Fla. 5th DCA 1997). However, a private organization does
not become a "surrogate public" entity or is not acting on behalf of a public entity merely because
it has a contract with a public entity. News and Sun-Sentinel Co.. et al.. v. Schwab. Twitty & Hanser
Architectural Group. Inc.. etc.. et aI., 596 So.2d 1029 (Fla. 1992).
9. Communications Between a Board Member and an Alternate on the Same Board:
Pursuant to Attorney General Opinion 88-45, such communications do not come under the
jurisdiction of the Sunshine Law.
10. State Legislature: All legislative committee meetings must be open to the public (but
the Governor, cabinet, and legislature itself are not agencies per the public records law).
11. Staff Meetings: These are normally exempt from the Sunshine Law except where given
a designated function which is outside its normal functions and part of the decision making process.
The Attorney General has ruled that an "agenda preparation group" which limited itself to fact
finding and developing back up for the agenda was not subject to the Sunshine Law. However,

function staff must operate in the Sunshine.

12. Commissioners Attending Advisory Board Meetings: City Commissioners may choose

to attend and even participate in public discussion at an advisory board. This issue was the subject

of the following question to and response from the Attorney General:

If a proposed ordinance is referred by the city commission to the city

A city commission may attend the meeting of a city advisory board; commissioners may

13. Community Organizations: Commissioners may attend forums sponsored by private

14. Circulating Memoranda: Merely circulating a memo from a board member to inform

matters which would be decided by the commission at a later date.

IV. APPLICABILITY: TO WHAT DOES THE LAW APPLY?

1. Discussions: Sometimes mere discussions can constitute decision making for the purpose
of the Sunshine Law. As indicated earlier in Krause. id., an advisory board of citizens reporting to
the City Manager on the selection of a chief of police was subject to the Sunshine Law.
It appears to be the nature of the act which defines whether the discussion is one to which
the Sunshine Law applies. Where the discussion forms any part ofthe policy making function, even
if advisory and non-binding, it will likely be held to the Sunshine Law.
2. Fact Finding: The previously cited Carlson case considered whether a staff which
,

prepared a recommended budget for a hospital district was subject to the Sunshine Law. It was
found that the committee played a significant part in the decision making process and thus its
deliberations must be conducted in the Sunshine. The Attorney General's Office has ruled that an
ad hoc committee that was appointed by a mayor to discuss a proposed transfer of city property was
subject to the Sunshine Law. (AGO 86-51). In AGO 85-76, a committee appointed by a mayor for
the purpose of making recommendations concerning legislation was also held to be subject to the
Sunshine Law.
However, again, we find the distinction as supporting a determination that certain actions are
not subject to the Sunshine Law. Ifthe committee is constituted merely for the purpose of finding
certain facts and reporting on said facts, the Sunshine Law may be held not to apply. Cape
Publications. Inc. v. CitvofPalmBay. supra; Bennettv. Worden, 333 SI, 2d 86 (Fla. 2dDCA 1976).
3. What is a Meeting? Any gathering of two or more members ofthe same agency at which
"foreseeable action" will be taken, will be considered to be a meeting for the purposes of the
Sunshine Law. Hough v. Stembridge, supra. In Times Publishing Company v. Williams, supra, the
court went as far as to determine that "every thought. as well as every affirmative act. of a public
official as it relates to and is within the scope of his official duties is a matter of public concern. . .
and is the entire decision making process that the legislature intended to effect by the enactment of
the Statute." See Hough. at 473. Thus, workshop meetings, conference sessions, negotiating
sessions and various other deliberations will be found to be subject to the Sunshine Law. (AGO 74-
94; AGO 74-358; AGO 73-8; AGO 74-354).

4. Meetings With Legal Counsel: The legislature has finally adopted a procedure to allow
members of boards to meet with their legal counsel (Section 286.011[8]). The subject must be
pending litigation to which the agency is presently a party (it may be a judicial or administrative
proceeding). The exception requires that a public meeting first be held. at which time legal counsel
states that he/she wishes to provide advice concerning litigation. The meeting may only concern
settlement negotiations or strategy. The agency and its attorney may then meet privately, but only
after the chairman estimates the length of the private meeting and lists the persons attending. The
City Commission, its attorney, and the City Manager are the only ones who may attend (AGO 95-
06). A court reporter must transcribe the entire private session (which transcript will be made public
at the conclusion ofthe litigation). At the close ofthe private session, the members shall reopen the
public meeting for the sole purpose of terminating the meeting. Notice of the meeting should be
posted.
In the case of Brown v. Lauderhill, 654 So.2d 302 (Fla. 4th DCA 1995) a Sunshine Law
violation was alleged after the City Commission met with its attorney to discuss an action by the
Mayor to recover attorney's fees from one who filed an unsuccessful and frivolous ethics complaint.
The Plaintiff argued that the City was not a party, thus the exception did not apply. The court ruled
that since the Mayor was seeking reimbursement fees expended by the City (for payment back to the
City) that, for all practical purposes, the City was a real party in interest. The court also noted that
the City would soon be involved in any litigation to protect its interest in reimbursement for its
interest in the fee. In a recent case the Mayor failed to list all persons attending a shade meeting.
The court ruled that to be a violation of the statute.
5. Labor Negotiations: Collective bargaining negotiations with a city manager or strong
mayor and the bargaining agent are subject to the Sunshine Law. However, there is provision for
the holding of an executive session where the chief executive officer of the governing body wishes
to meet with the governing body to discuss the status of collective bargaining negotiations or
strategy. (Section 447.605[1]). The City must actively be engaged in collective bargaining. Note
that bargaining sessions must be conducted in the Sunshine.
6. Candidate Forums: This has been the subject of much controversy in recent years. The
Attorney General has informally stated that candidate forums and debates do not fall within the

7. Negotiations: Where the governing body, either by specific action or by acquiescence,
permits a city official to negotiate on its behalfthere is always a possibility that the Sunshine Law
could apply. The fact that the governing body must still affirm the results of the negotiations may
not be a sufficient remedy to obviate the requirement for Sunshine Law procedures. Where it is a
normal function of a manager, department head, or legal counsel to negotiate the contract, there
would be no violation; the danger occurs where a commissioner is formally anointed with authority.
8. Complaint Review Boards: Such boards are subject to the Sunshine Law.
v. LITIGATION ISSUES
1. As indicated earlier, in any litigation involving the Sunshine Law, one should be prepared
for courts to place a liberal construction upon the law. The law is considered to be a benefit to the .
public, thus, the application of the law will be liberally construed and exemptions narrowly
construed. Board of Public Instruction of Broward County v. Duran, supra.
2. In any civil complaints involving the Sunshine Law, the Fourth District Court of Appeal
has held that the identity of the public officials involved must be alleged. As we have discussed, it
generally takes two public officials to constitute a public meeting. Therefore, any party wishing to
institute a civil suit should be prepared to allege the names of the officials which are claimed to have
been involved in the violation. Deerfield Publishing. Inc. v. Robb. 537 So.2d 510 (Fla. 4th DCA
1988).
3. Injunctive Relief: Injunctive relief is available under the Sunshine Law. Normally
irreparable injury is required to obtain an injunction. However, in Sunshine Law cases such a
showing is not required upon proofthat the law has been violated. Town of Palm Beach v. Gradison,
supra. As indicated, the complaint seeking injunctive relief must name or sufficiently describe the
identity of the public officials involved.

2006 PUBLIC RECORDS LAW UPDATE
I. THE LAW: CHAPTER 119
1. "It is the policy of this State that all state, county, and municipal records shall at all times
be open for a personal inspection by any person". (Section 119.01, Florida Statutes).
2. Public records are defined as all "documents, papers, letters, maps, books, tapes,
photographs, films, sound recordings or other material, regardless of physical form or characteristics
made or received pursuant to law or ordinance or in connection with the transaction of official
business by any agency". (Section 119.011, Florida Statutes). E-mail is included. (AGO 96-34).
3. A public record is a document prepared in connection with official business to
communicate, perpetuate, or formalize information. Handwritten notes not meant to be distributed
or filed for a record should not constitute a public record.
4. The law requires that all public records be available for inspection and examination by
any person desiring said inspection, at any reasonable time, under reasonable conditions, and under
supervision by the custodian ofthe public records, or his designee. The law also requires that the
custodian of the records shall furnish a copy or certified copy of the record upon payment of the
appropriate fee. If there is no fee established by legislation, the public agency may charge the actual
cost of the duplication of the record with a maximum of fifteen cents per legal size page (and an
additional five cents per double-sided copy). Ifthere is no fee established, the local governing body
is not permitted to charge for the cost of labor or overhead as part of the cost of duplication.
II. APPLICABILITY: TO WHOM DOES THE LAW APPLY?
1. The law applies to any agency. It defines "Agency" as any state, county, district,
authority, or municipal officer, department, division board, bureau, commission, or other separate
unit of government created or established by law and any other public or private agency, person,
partnership, corporation, or business entity acting on behalf of any public agency. (Section 119.011,
Florida Statutes).
2. Advisory Boards: Any advisory board created by a publicly created agency is considered
an "agency" for the purposes ofthe Public Records Law.
3. Private Corporations: This will be decided on a case-by-case basis. Merely contracting
with a public agency will not cause a private corporation to be subject to the Public Records Law.

Parsons and Whittemore, Inc. v. Metropolitan Dade County, 429 So. 2d 343 (Fla. 3rd DCA 1983).
To the extent a private corporation or entity performs public or governmental responsibilities and
functions on behalf of an "agency," it may be subj ect to the Public Records Law. A purely volunteer
fire department which provided the sole fire fighting capability for an "agency" was found to be
subject to the Public Records Law. Schwartzman v. Merritt Island Volunteer Fire Department, 352
So.2d (Fla. 4th DCA 1977). Ithas been held that a towing company which performs towing services
for a city pursuant to a contract for towing abandoned automobiles from public streets is subject to
the Public Records Law, Fox v. News-Press Publishinf! Company. Inc., 545 So.2d 941 (Fla. 2nd
DCA 1989). Moreover, a consulting engineer who performed the services of a city engineer relative
to a water treatment plant was also held to be an "agency" under the terms of Chapter 119 and, thus,
subject to the Public Records Law. Fritz v. Norflor Construction Company. 386 So.2d 899 (Fla. 5th
DCA 1980). The Shands Teaching Hospital was found not to be an "agency" under the Public
Records Law because of the lack of day-to-day control exercised by a public agency. Campus
Communications, Inc. v. Shands Teaching Hospital and Clinics. Inc., 512 So.2d 999 (Fla 1st DCA
1987). The case of Sarasota Herald Tribune v. Community Health C01]Joration, 582 So.2d 730 (2nd
DCA 1991) contains an excellent discussion of this subject. The courts will look to how the private
agency was created (i.e. who created it - the government agency), how it receives its funding, the
purpose for which the agency was created, ownership interest in the assets, the degree of interrelation
with the government agency, and the amount of control over the private agency.
A recent ruling that handing over a document to an outside attorney does not exempt it from
disclosure under the public records law. The key issue is the nature of the document.
III. APPLICABILITY: TO WHAT DOES THE LAW APPLY?
1. Personnel Records: With few exceptions, personnel records must be open for inspection.
Wait v. Florida Power & Light Company, 372 So. 420 (Fla. 1979); Shevin. etc.. et al.. v. Byron.
Harless. Schaffer, Reid and Associates. Inc., 379 So.2d 634 (Fla. 1980). There are certain medical
records which are exempted from this requirement for public inspection. These include medical
records and claim records of current or former state, county, or municipal employees and eligible
dependents enrolled in group insurance plans. A City may not maintain personnel reyords in

separate files, one of which is open and one of which is confidential. A city cannot remove records
from a personnel file or conceal them in other files (AGO 73-51; AGO 94-54).
2. Notes and Memoranda: Any document prepared in connection with government business,
which document is aimed at perpetuating, communicating or formalizing knowledge, will be
considered a public record subject to inspection. Shevin, id. This will include interoffice
memoranda, preliminary drafts of proposed ordinances or regulations, and preliminary drafts of
reports. The Attorney General has determined that inter-office memoranda, correspondence,
inspection reports, and other documents maintained by county public health units are public records
and subj ect to inspection in the absence of any statutory exemption or confidentiality requirement.
(AGO 85-79).
Personnel notes and drafts often fall in a different category. If the personal notes are intended
to perpetuate or formalize knowledge, they would be considered public record. Alternatively, the
Attorney General has opined that personal, uncirculated notes, which form the early stage of a
document which will later be circulated or kept as evidence of governmental action are not public
records.
A court has recently held that a draft audit report ofthe Clerk ofthe Circuit Court acting as
the county's auditor was not subject to the public records law. This is peculiar to audit reports.
3. Disciplinary Action: Records and materials related to investigations of employee conduct
and disciplinary actions are public records unless there is a specific exemption related to the
substance of a particular investigation. Caswel v. Manhattan Fire & Marine Insurance Company,
399 F. 2d 417 (5th Cir. 1968). There are specific exemptions for criminal investigations, complaints
against school teachers (prior to the conclusion of investigation), and complaints against police
officers (until the conclusion of the internal investigation).
4. Arrest and crime reports are public records except to the extent they disclose active
criminal investigative information.
5. Attorney/Client Communications: Letters and memoranda between attorneys and
government agencies are subject to the Public Records Law. (However, see exceptions for attorney
work product).

1. Waiting Periods: These are not permitted under Chapter 119, Florida Statutes. Tribune

A "reasonable time" is allowed for a response.

3. How Must Information Be Supplied: A citizen is not permitted to request that the

to the actual cost of duplication. This does not include incidental expenses such as utilities,
personnel and office expenses. In cases where the nature ofthe request is extremely voluminous and

for obtaining reimbursement for such direct costs. (Section 119.07[1], Florida Statutes). Absent

SUBJECT: Robert's Rules of Order

The most commonly used form of procedural rules at governmental meetings
is Robert's Rules of Order. There is also a very misunderstood document with many
arcane rules and concepts. The purpose of this, very brief, memo is to summarize
some of the more important points in Robert's Rules of Order in order to facilitate the
effective administration of board meetings.

Business should be brought before the board by use of a motion made by any
member of the board. There are four classes of motions, main motions, subsidiary
motions, incidental motions, and privileged motions. The main motion has the
lowest rank among motions, followed by the subsidiary, incidental and then the
privileged motion. The privileged motion has the highest status. This surprises
many people inasmuch as the main motion is always the first step in any board
activity. It is generally the motion that disposes of a matter. However, it is inferior
to almost every other type of a motion. It is also important to understand that once
the maker of a motion makes a motion and it is seconded, the maker loses control
over the motion. That is, technically, the motion cannot even be amended by the
maker without permission of the board. Further, it cannot be withdrawn without
permission of the board. Notwithstanding these technicalities, many boards allow
the chairman to accept minor amendments and withdrawals by general consent of
the board. Without said general consent, it would take another motion for the
makers in order to change or withdraw the motion.

1

<<PG>>

Subsidiary motions are motions such as a motion to table, to limit debate, to
postpone indefinitely, or to amend. Incidental motions are points of order, appeals,
and motions to object as a consideration of a question and to suspend the rules and
to withdraw a modified motion. Privileged motions are motions to adjourn, recess,
and raise a question of privilege.

I will discuss below some of the most common motions:

MOTION TO TABLE

The motion to table is a motion to remove the main question from
consideration for a period of time. Technically it is not in order when another
member has the floor, it does require a second, it is not debatable or amendable.
It requires a majority vote.

MOTION TO CALL THE QUESTION

A motion to call the question can also be thought of as a motion to limit
debate. The motion to call the question takes precedence over the main motion, but
yields to a motion to table. It requires a second and is not debatable. It requires a
2/3 vote to be adopted. If adopted, the question must be voted on.

MOTION TO AMEND

The motion to amend takes precedence over nothing but the motion it
proposes to amend. It requires a second and is debatable.

MOTION TO POSTPONE INDEFINITELY

This is different from a motion to table in that it opens to debate the entire
main motion that is proposed to postpone indefinitely. It is superior only to the
motion it proposes to postpone.

MOTION TO RECONSIDER

The motion to reconsider has the effect of placing before the board the original
. question in the exact condition that it was before it was voted upon. In fact, it has
the effect of nullifying the vote on the main motion. A motion to reconsider must be
seconded and must be made by a member of the prevailing side. Generally the rule
is that it may also be made by someone who did not vote on the question. This

2

<<PG>>

motion is usually required to be considered only at the next meeting of the board.
The motion may be seconded by someone who is not on the prevailing side. The
motion can be debated.

This is a very brief summary of some of the many motions that are available. .
I believe these are the most commonly made motions. There are many other rules
of parliamentary procedure, however, it is generally a bad sign when we have to
resort to them. Most meetings are reasonably collegial and with the consent of the
entire board, in a cooperative manner, most of the business of the board can be
conducted through the use of the above referenced devices. Of course, if you have
any questions, please do not hesitate to contact me and we can go into even more
depth.
