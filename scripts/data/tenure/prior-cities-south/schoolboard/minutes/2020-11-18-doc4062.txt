Superintendent Carvalho explained that currently there were three
Board approved COVID-19 testing sites located in school parking lots.
Very soon they would be announcing a partnership with community
organizations, such as University of Miami Pediatrics, in addition to the
7,200 rapid tests obtained from the state, to provide free mobile
coronavirus tests for students.
Continuing, he said contact tracing was not a function of the school
system, but rather the Department of Health. The county provided all
the funding for the approximately 400 contact tracing investigators in
place. The District relied on those individuals to engage in investigation
and notification of entities with direct or indirect contact to presumed
cases.
Finally, the Superintendent explained that the call to quarantine or
closure of a school was made in careful consultation with the
Department of Health, the Department of Education, and taking into
account a whole host of local considerations, such as the number of
students impacted, the number of employees impacted and the nature
of their position—were they stationary or roving within one or more
schools—the timing of notification, the mitigation of the process, the
consultation with the Department of Health, and the communication
protocols activated. He expressed understanding for the anxiety and
confusion of the community, as there was no specific equation with
absolute known variables that produced a number by which to close a
school. In closing, he said they had the desire to be as responsive,
transparent, and diligent in the implementation of best practices, and
this item put them in that direction.
In response to a request from Ms. Santos for further clarification on the
action of this item, Dr. Gallon said that although staff may be proactively
working on addressing these issues, a response would need to come
back to the Board in the form of a report on December 2 to satiate the
action of this item.
With regards to recommended action number five, Dr. Gallon said that

 Monitoring Academic Performance during COVID-19
In his introduction, Superintendent Carvalho said there was a
national fear associated with academic regression; learning loss as
a result of a disrupted fourth quarter last school year, followed by
the compound effect of the academic regression many students
experience during the summer, and the additional academic
regression many students may be going through as a result of un-
engagement on the part of students. The students most at risk were
does in crisis even before the COVID crisis struck; students of color,
students in poverty, homeless students, students in foster care,
migrant children, students with disabilities.
Continuing, he said that the academic progress of students had
always been a priority for this Board and administration. Their ability
to dramatically increase student outcomes – despite what may be
expected given this system’s size and the diverse population– had
garnered this system national and international recognition. COVID-
19, undoubtedly, had a profound impact on everyone’s daily lives –
changing the way we live and work. It also had a significant impact
on education and what students and staff experience in teaching
and learning. Speaker comments today served as testimonial to
that—everything has changed.
During this time of great uncertainty in this community, the school
district responded rapidly and decisively in its transition to distance
learning during the last quarter of the 2019-2020 academic year
when COVID-19 forced to close schools in March of 2020. Despite
their ability to execute on this, they began to worry about the impact
prolonged school closures would have on students’ ability to learn,
particularly the most fragile students. Many students across the
country, including here in Miami-Dade, either started the 2020-2021
school year via remote means or were currently engaging in
distance learning, understandably as a result of efforts to mitigate
the spread of COVID-19 and parent choice. In fact, this past Friday,
U.S. News and World Report indicated that approximately 65% of
U.S. households with children have used online learning during the
pandemic. About that same percentage – 65% -- have had contact
with a teacher at least four days per week. However, 11% of
households with children, representing approximately 5.5 million
children, had no contact with a teacher in the past week. These
national numbers regarding engagement – or really, the lack thereof
– were very concerning. Moreover, they were but one indicator of
what many school systems would be forced to contend with for the
years to come – significant student regression as a result of the
impact COVID-19 has had on the education of youth.
This administration had begun to monitor, analyze, and strategize
about this since schools first closed in March; efforts in this arena
had only continued to increase in the new school year in light of the
number of students participating in My School Online and the state
of COVID-19 within the local community, which had inherently
impacted our schools and has led to a certain level of disruption to
the learning process for affected students. He also noted that this
