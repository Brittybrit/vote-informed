Project Description

Devil’s Garden includes 82,955 acres in Hendry and Collier Counties. The project is named after
a wetland slough in the eastern portion of the overall project. The western portion, the focus of
this acquisition, is comprised of a mosaic of natural lands used primarily for cattle grazing.

Landscape connectivity with other conservation lands, including federal ownership, ensures
longevity for a wide-range of species including the Florida Panther, and provides opportunities for
wetlands and watershed protections. Okaloacoochee Slough is adjacent to the western boundary
of the subject property and is part of the Great Florida Birding and Wildlife Trail. There are
expanded recreational opportunities for visitors to access the Okaloacoochee Slough Wildlife
Management Area and the adjacent State Forest.

The connectivity of uplands and wetlands draining both to the north and south are important to
protecting state water resources. The natural systems of Fakahatchee Strand and Big Cypress
Preserve are dependent on the water supplied from this area, and a majority of the Devil’s Garden
project area lies within the Western Everglades Restoration project planning boundary.

The long-term stewardship of the landowner is evident through the diversity of ecosystems and
resource integrity found throughout the western portion of the project area. We anticipate working
in this basin on future acquisitions in the Devil’s Garden project.

Property Description

The subject property is 5,534 acres; comprised of 3,233 acres of uplands and 2,301 acres of
wetlands. There is a general slope to the subject property from the southwest towards the northeast
and east, with the subject property waterflow generally running northward. The northward water
flow leads to a canal that runs east-west and then moves north toward the Caloosahatchee River.

The subject property is adjacent to the northeastern boundary of the Okaloacoochee Slough State
Forest and the eastern boundary of the Okaloacoochee Slough Wildlife Management Area, and
serves as a primary and secondary zone for the federally endangered Florida panther. Numerous
records of panther use, as well as other rare and threatened plants and animals, have been noted
throughout the property. Acquiring the subject property will contribute to increased protection of
Florida’s biodiversity at the species, natural community, and landscape levels.

There are three hunt camps on the subject property that are considered primitive structures and
have no contributory value to the subject property. The Department of Environmental Protection’s
(DEP) Division of State Lands and the managing agency, Florida Fish and Wildlife Conservation
Commission (FWC), will determine if the structures will be removed by the owner prior to closing.
