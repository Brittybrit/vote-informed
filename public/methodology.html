Ballot305.org

How it works

Ballot305 is a free, independent research tool for Miami-Dade voters. It is built by one Miami Beach resident, not by a campaign, party, or government office. This page explains where the information comes from, how labels are assigned, and what the tool can get wrong.

Where the research comes from

When you tap a candidate or ballot measure, the site runs an AI research assistant (Anthropic's Claude) that searches the web and reads what it finds. It is told to report only what sources say, keep each source's hedges, and link every claim. Links it did not actually find in search are removed automatically.

  • Federal races (U.S. Senate, Congress): donor lists come from official Federal Election Commission filings.
  • State races (Governor and Cabinet, State Senate and House, State Attorney, Public Defender, judges): donors come straight from contributions reported to the Florida Division of Elections for the 2026 election, refreshed daily. Loans and in-kind contributions (goods or services instead of cash) are noted.
  • County races (County Commission, School Board, county judges, community councils): donors come straight from contributions reported to the Miami-Dade Elections Department, refreshed daily. When a candidate amends a report, the corrections are applied, so totals match the county's own published figures.
  • City races file with each city clerk. Coral Gables, Cutler Bay, Doral and Hialeah publish every contribution in the same online system the county uses, so those candidates' donors come straight from the clerk's records, with amended reports applied. Key Biscayne, Miami Gardens, North Miami, Opa-locka, Palmetto Bay and Sunny Isles Beach use a different online system (EasyVote Campaign Finance); donors there also come straight from the clerk's records, each linked to the report that lists the gift. That system itemizes only reports filed electronically, so a candidate who filed scans links to the report itself. Some reports cover overlapping dates or relist earlier gifts, so a gift that appears in more than one report is counted once. Totals include loans and in-kind gifts. Aventura, El Portal, Medley, North Miami Beach, Pinecrest, South Miami and Virginia Gardens post only scanned reports, so their candidates' donors were typed in from each report and checked against the report's own totals; they cover the reports the clerk had posted when last updated. Where a report's itemized gifts don't add up to its own totals, or a candidate posted only a summary page, the candidate's page says so. Other cities post scanned PDFs or nothing online, so their candidates' donors come from news and web sources, and coverage is uneven.
  • When many people gave the same amount at the cutoff (often the legal limit), the page says how many gave that much, since which of them appear in the top list is arbitrary.
  • Endorsements are linked to the endorser's own announcement or to news coverage of it.
  • Ratings such as bar association polls are shown only when found on the rating organization's own website.

Each result is saved and shared for up to 7 days, so the next voter to look up the same candidate sees the same research. That keeps the site free to run and means everyone sees the same thing.

Donors

Money given directly to a candidate's campaign is listed separately from money given to political committees that support them. Florida limits direct gifts to a campaign ($3,000 per election for statewide offices, $1,000 for most others), so larger amounts went to a committee. For federal candidates, the list includes the candidate's own fundraising committees, since that money funds their campaign.

How local is the money

Each candidate gets two panels: supporting money (campaign donations plus outside groups spending to help) and opposition money (outside groups spending to defeat them). Every itemized gift is placed by the address on the official filing: inside the area that votes for the office, elsewhere in Miami-Dade, elsewhere in Florida, outside Florida, or not reported. People, groups (PACs, companies, unions, parties) and the candidate’s own money are shown separately.

Statewide races count all of Florida. Countywide races and Miami-Dade’s own circuit count Miami-Dade, by ZIP code. City races count addresses whose mailing city is that city, which misses homes inside the city with a “Miami” mailing address. District races (Congress, the Legislature, County Commission, School Board, city districts) are matched to the county or city around the district, not the district itself, and say “located in” that place rather than “in the area that votes for this candidate.” Florida redrew its congressional districts in 2026. Some districts also reach into Broward or Monroe; donors there are counted as elsewhere in Florida. City matches use the mailing city, which many homes inside a city list as “Miami,” so city shares are approximate.

A group is placed by the address it filed under, often its treasurer’s or a Tallahassee office, not by where its own donors live. For federal races, the FEC gives a location only for itemized gifts from people; gifts it doesn’t itemize and all PAC and party money show as not reported. Outside spenders are placed by the address they registered with the FEC. This guide doesn’t yet total spending for or against state and local candidates (groups file those reports with the Florida Division of Elections or the county or city clerk), so those races have no opposition panel. Outside spending is shown in its own row, separate from groups that gave to the campaign. Cities that post only scanned reports have no addresses to count.

Donors, lobbyists and official actions

This section has two parts. The first lists official actions by an incumbent on items that name one of their campaign donors as the applicant, contractor, lobbyist’s client or other party: votes, motions, items they presented, and votes they missed. Each one is checked by hand against the meeting minutes or roll call, and shows the full vote, not only the incumbent’s. Unanimous and consent-agenda votes are included, because influence can show up before a vote is taken: in who brings an item forward, who makes the motion, and whether a donor funds every member. Donation dates are shown as recorded. A gift before or after an action is listed, not judged, and no item here claims a vote was bought. When a donor is a sister company of the party in the item (a different LLC name), it is included only when state corporate records show a shared address and shared managers or registered agent, and the card says what was and was not confirmed.

Each card also lists the registered lobbyists on the item, when the body keeps a lobbyist registry that can be checked: the lobbyist, their firm, the client as the registry writes it, and the registration date (some registries give only the month and year). A lobbyist’s registration date before or after the vote is shown as recorded. Items a registered lobbyist worked on are included even when no one involved gave to the candidate; those cards say so. In Aventura and North Miami Beach the review started from the city’s own registry: every land-use approval, contract and appointment a registered client asked for from 2022 on was found in the signed resolutions (Aventura) or the minutes (North Miami Beach), and the incumbent’s vote recorded. Many of those records are scans; they were read with text recognition and each vote checked against the page. North Miami Beach’s minutes also record each member’s sworn disclosure of who they spoke with before land-use votes, and cards quote it when names are given. Where a registration and a resolution spell the client differently, both spellings are shown and they are not treated as the same company without a source that says so. Registries used: Miami-Dade County, Coral Gables, Doral, Aventura (list of May 6, 2026), North Miami Beach (2023, 2024 and Aug. 13, 2026 lists), and the lobbyists named on state budget request forms.

Every candidate in a city race, and in County Commission District 5, gets a status line when there are no actions to show: not a current member of that body; a current member whose actions were reviewed and none named a donor; a current member whose itemized reports are not available, so the check can’t be done; a current member whose reports list no outside donors; or a current member not yet reviewed. A review searches the posted minutes, agendas and resolutions for the names of the member’s business, PAC and larger individual donors. It covers what each city has posted online, which differs by city, so “none found” means none in the records searched. All of it, city by city, is on the Donors, lobbyists and official actions page.

State legislators don’t vote on contracts and permits the way city councils do, so for Florida House and Senate incumbents on the ballot the list covers two other kinds of action. First, state budget project requests they filed (House Appropriations Project Request forms and Senate Local Funding Initiative Request forms for the 2025 and 2026 sessions) where the organization receiving the money gave to them, or where the lobbying firm named on the form as the registered lobbyist, its PAC, or its lobbyists gave to them. Requests lobbied by the same firm are grouped into one card that lists each project. Second, bills they filed where a donor is a clear beneficiary; the card says how the donor is tied to the bill and does not claim the donor asked for it. Donations come from the Florida Division of Elections for 2025 and 2026, and include gifts to a political committee the legislator chairs, labeled as such. Every request form these legislators filed for the 2025 and 2026 sessions was read. Whether a request was funded in the final budget was not checked.

The second part lists donors who gave to more than one candidate. For a city race it compares every race in that city on the ballot; otherwise, the candidates in the same race. It uses the official county and city reports (Miami-Dade Elections, VoterFocus and EasyVote city portals, and the city reports typed in from scans). Givers are matched by name, ignoring endings like “Inc.” or “LLC” and anything after “d/b/a.” Companies, PACs and unions are also grouped when they list the same street address, which is how one developer’s many LLCs show up as one giver. People are never grouped by address, since family members share homes. A shared address does not prove shared ownership. Federal and state races are not compared here yet.

Starting from the vote

The official actions by vote page works the other way around. It starts from what the Miami-Dade County Commission decided, not from a candidate’s donors. Every item the commission acted on from November 18, 2025 (when Commissioner Vicki L. Lopez was seated) through October 6, 2026 was searched for a named private company, developer or group that gets a contract, lease, county land, zoning approval, money or a change to an existing deal. Each item shows the full roll call from the Clerk of the Board’s minutes (the motion, the tally, each “no” and each absence), checked against the roll call in the signed resolution where the clerk posts one; on all 72 items with both, they match. It then lists everyone the county’s lobbyist registry shows for that company in 2025 or 2026, with the registration date. Only the two commissioners on the November 3 ballot have their donors checked: Lopez (her county campaign and the Common Sense Government political committee she chairs) and Oliver G. Gilbert III (his federal campaign for U.S. House). A gift is listed when the giver is the company itself; when the giver has the same name as a registered lobbyist on the item and the report shows the same address or a lobbying-type job; or when the giver is one of four lobbying firms (Bercow Radell Fernandez Larkin & Tapanes, Capital City Consulting, LSN Partners, Pantin Gov) at the same office address as a registered lobbyist on the item. Each gift says which of these applies. Other companies at the same address as an applicant are not counted, because a shared address does not show who owns them. State legislators will be added next.

Cities follow the same steps where the records allow. In Coral Gables, every commission item from January 2025 on was pulled from the city’s Legistar system with each member’s recorded vote; the list keeps items whose agenda names a lobbyist or whose company is in the city’s 2024-2026 lobbyist registrations, plus items whose company gave to a commissioner on the ballot. A registration counts if it is in the list for the year of the vote or was filed within the year before, and not withdrawn before the vote. In Doral, every council meeting’s minutes from January 2025 on were read; Doral posts only its current lobbyist list (with each lobbyist’s clients), so a 2025 item can show a lobbyist registered after the vote. In Aventura and North Miami Beach the list starts from the cities’ own registries, as described above. North Miami, South Miami, Pinecrest, Opa-locka and Medley post no lobbyist registry we could find, so their lists start from items that name a donor of an official on the ballot, and each gift carries a note on how it connects. Each city’s section on the page says which of these applies. Where the minutes say only that a vote was unanimous, the members shown are the ones seated at the time, taken from roll calls in the same period; where that can’t be confirmed, only the names the record gives are shown.

For the Florida Legislature, the actions are the budget requests that each Miami-Dade legislator on the ballot filed: every House Appropriations Project Request for the 2025 and 2026 sessions and every Senate Local Funding Initiative Request for fiscal years 2025–26 and 2026–27. Each form names the member who filed it, the group that would receive the money and the registered lobbyist working on it, if any. A request is shown with a gift when the group receiving the money, the lobbying firm named on the form, that firm’s political committee or one of that firm’s lobbyists gave to the member’s campaign account or to a political committee the member chairs, from January 2025 on, at any amount. Lobbyist names are as the form spells them. Each one is checked against the Legislature’s 2024, 2025 and 2026 lobbyist registrations (the lists by client, downloaded October 2026): the request says whether that lobbyist is registered for a client with the same name as the group on the form, is registered only for other clients, or is not in any of the three lists. Client names are matched when they are the same apart from words like “Inc.” or “Foundation”; where the registry spells a client differently, both spellings are shown. Whether a request was funded in the final budget was not checked. There is no roll call for a request, so only the member who filed it is shown.

City transparency check

The city transparency check rates each city on the ballot against eight basics, three about campaign money (current reports posted, past reports posted, donors searchable as data) and five about meetings (agendas with backup documents, minutes posted, votes recorded by name, searchable text, video). The first two follow Miami-Dade County Code section 12-7, which since 2023 requires each city to post its candidates’ campaign finance reports within 7 days and keep its previous two elections’ reports up. Each rating is yes, partly or no, checked by hand against the city’s own websites and the official systems they link to, and links the page it rests on. It shows what a voter can find online, so a “no” means we could not find it, not proof that a record does not exist.

Data coverage and open records requests

Official donor records are not available the same way everywhere. Federal, state and county candidates file in public databases, and the app reads every contribution from them. City candidates file with each city clerk, and cities publish those reports in different ways, or not at all. Miami-Dade County Code §12-7 (Ordinance 23-63, 2023) requires every city in the county to post its current candidates' campaign finance reports on its website within 7 days of receiving them. It doesn't require a searchable format, so a scanned PDF counts.

Where a city's official records aren't available to the app, a candidate's donors come from news and web research, and the candidate's page says so. Where we couldn't find a city's reports online, we asked the clerk for them in a public records request. This table shows where each request stands.

Outside spending

Since the Supreme Court's 2010 Citizens United decision, groups can spend unlimited money on their own ads, mail and texts for or against a candidate, as long as they don't coordinate with the campaign. This money never goes to the campaign, so it is not listed as a donation. It is shown in its own Outside Spending section, split into money spent to help the candidate and money spent to defeat them.

  • Federal races: amounts come from FEC filings, including the 24- and 48-hour notices groups must file close to an election. Spending already counted in a regular report is not counted again. Only spending for the November general election is shown; money spent in the primary is left out. Each group reports whether its spending supports or opposes the candidate, so that label is the group’s own, and the few lines filed without one are left out. Outside spending is checked against the FEC daily. For each registered PAC or super PAC there is a link to its own donor list.
  • State races: we search the Florida Division of Elections for spending by committees and parties since January 1, 2025 whose filed purpose names the candidate, and check it daily. Florida filings have no field for supporting or opposing, so a side is shown only when the filing says it (“opposing,” “in support of”). The candidates’ own campaigns, contributions and transfers between committees are left out, since that money shows up on the receiving committee’s report. Spending that doesn’t name the candidate can’t be found this way, so these figures are a floor, not a total.
  • County and city races: outside spending is found through news coverage and county and city filings. This is less complete than federal or state data.

About each group

Every organization in a money list (campaign donors, committee donors, outside spenders, ballot measure funders, and the funders of those groups) has an "About this group" panel. Funders shown inside a panel have their own panels, so you can follow money from a PAC to the PAC that funds it and on to the nonprofit behind that. A group is matched to a federal committee or IRS nonprofit only on an exact name match. When there is no match, the panel says so and links to searches of Florida business records, IRS nonprofit records and FEC committees.

Florida political committees (state PACs and similar groups) are matched by exact name in the Florida Division of Elections committee database. The panel shows the committee's status, chair, treasurer and registered agent, how much it has reported raising in 2025–2026, and its top funders in that period from the state's contribution records. A company that once registered a committee under its own name (a closed committee with no recent activity) is treated as the company, not that old committee. When a Florida committee shows up as a donor in FEC records, the panel compares the city in the FEC record with the committee's address and says whether they match, so a different group with the same name isn't mistaken for it.

For federal committees, the panel is built from its own FEC filings:

  • Which side its money helped. Calculated from every candidate the group spent for or against this election cycle: the share that helped Republicans or opposed Democrats, versus the reverse. A group is labeled left or right only when 80% or more of its spending went one way. This is a count of its spending, not a judgment about the group.
  • When it formed and where it is based, from its FEC registration.
  • How much it raised and spent on outside spending across all races, as of its latest FEC report.
  • Its top funders. Super PACs must report their donors. When a top funder is a 501(c)(4), (c)(5) or (c)(6) nonprofit on the IRS list (checked through ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer, exact name matches only), it is marked "Donors not disclosed." LLC funders are marked "Owners not listed."
  • Who runs the nonprofits. A nonprofit doesn't have to name its donors, but it does have to list its officers, directors and key employees, with their pay, on its annual IRS Form 990. For nonprofit funders and nonprofit outside groups, the panel shows those people, the group's revenue and its location from its latest 990, through ProPublica's Nonprofit Explorer.
  • What the money bought in this race, such as TV ads, mail, or texts, from how the group described each expense to the FEC.

Super PACs file donor reports monthly or quarterly, so the newest money can take weeks to appear. Each panel shows the date its figures run through.

Money to allied committees

For state and county candidates, the candidate's own political committee (for example "Friends of [candidate]") is found among the campaign's donors in official filings. For each one, the page shows what it raised in 2025–2026, its largest donors with the date of each gift, and what it gave the campaign directly, all from the Florida Division of Elections. Florida committees report spending by who they paid (consultants, ad firms), not by which candidate it helped, so the rest of their spending for a candidate can't be totaled from state records. Committee donations found only in news or web sources are not shown, because they can’t be checked against filings and often lack a committee name or date.

Biggest backers

Near the top of each candidate and ballot measure page, "Biggest Backers Behind the Money" lists the people, companies and groups who gave the most to the committees and outside groups in that race, so they aren't buried inside nested panels. It starts from the outside groups spending for or against a candidate, the committees backing the candidate, or the committees for and against a measure. It takes each group's largest donors from official filings. When a donor is itself a PAC or political committee, it looks one step further back at that committee's largest donors. Each row shows the path the money took, for example: gave $X to Committee A, which gave $Y to Super PAC B, which spent $Z to help this candidate.

  • These people and companies gave to the groups, not to the candidate's campaign. Outside groups can't legally coordinate with campaigns.
  • Only gifts of $1,000 or more, and only each group's largest donors, so this is the top of the list, not all of it.
  • The amount next to each name is what they gave to the groups shown. Money passed through a committee isn't the same dollars arriving at the next group, so the path shows both amounts.
  • Nonprofits that don't disclose donors are where the public trail ends. They are listed as backers and marked "Donors not disclosed."

Time period

Endorsements, opposition and ratings must be for the November 2026 election. Ones from a candidate's earlier campaigns, even for the same office, are removed: anything dated before 2025 by the source or its link is dropped, and the rest is checked for signs of an earlier race. Group funders and totals cover the current two-year election cycle (2025–2026). Federal data uses the FEC's 2026 cycle. Florida committee data is limited to contributions dated January 1, 2025 or later. Candidate donors come from each candidate's filings for the November 2026 election. Donors, committee donors and outside spenders found through web research (where no official record was available) are held to the same rule: anything dated before 2025, or pointing to an earlier campaign, is removed.

Where the money trail stops

Some money can't be traced to the people who originally gave it. When that happens, the site says so plainly instead of hiding it:

DONORS NOT DISCLOSED
The group is a nonprofit, such as a 501(c)(4) or 501(c)(6), or an outside group that is not a registered PAC. These groups are not required to name their donors. This is legal and is used by groups across the political spectrum. A Public records link opens what is on file, such as its tax filings or FEC registration.
OWNERS NOT LISTED
The donor is a limited liability company (LLC). A donation in a company name doesn't show who owns the company. A Public records link opens its Florida business record, which lists its officers or managers.

Left, right, and nonpartisan labels

Every endorser, opponent, and funder except labor unions gets a label describing the group's general political alignment, not the candidate's:

LEFT RIGHT NONPARTISAN

Party organizations and ideological advocacy groups get the side they are aligned with. Sheriff and police chief associations, bar associations, and the League of Women Voters are always labeled nonpartisan. Newspapers and their editorial boards are labeled “News outlet” with no lean, since an editorial board can endorse candidates of either party.

The fixed list, applied the same way in every race (everything else is labeled by research):

  • Left: Democratic party, club, executive committee, caucus and women’s organizations; Young Democrats; College Democrats.
  • Right: Republican party, club, executive committee, caucus, Liberty Caucus and women’s organizations; Young Republicans; College Republicans; GOP.
  • Nonpartisan: sheriff and police chief associations, fire chiefs, bar associations, The Florida Bar, League of Women Voters.
  • News outlet: Miami Herald, el Nuevo Herald, Sun Sentinel, Palm Beach Post, Tampa Bay Times, Orlando Sentinel, Tallahassee Democrat, Florida Times-Union, Miami Today, Biscayne Times, Miami New Times, The Miami Times, WLRN, Axios, and any editorial board.
  • Labor unions: no left or right label; shown by the kind of workers they represent.

Labor unions get no left or right label. Instead they show the kind of workers they represent, such as TEACHERS FIREFIGHTERS or POLICE, plus a LABOR UNION tag. This applies the same way to every union, including police, firefighter and teacher unions and labor federations like the AFL-CIO.

Groups that appear in many races use one fixed label everywhere, so the same group is never labeled differently in two places. Labels for other groups are assigned during research and are a judgment call. If you think one is wrong, tell us (see below).

The Federalist Society screen (judges only)

The Federalist Society is a national organization of conservative and libertarian lawyers. Membership is legal and common, and it is not a mark against anyone. Voters asked to know about these ties because the group has played a large role in judicial appointments. Every judicial candidate gets one of three results:

DOCUMENTED TIES
The judge states their own membership or a chapter role, or the Federalist Society lists them as a member or officer, or records show them speaking at two or more of its events on different dates.
POSSIBLE TIES
One appearance at a Federalist Society event, or a named journalist, academic, or voter guide that asserts ties to this judge by name. Speaking at one event does not mean membership. Judges of every outlook speak at its events.
NO TIES FOUND
Nothing in searched coverage, bios, or event records connects the judge to the Federalist Society.

Evidence only counts if it names the judge. Articles about appointment trends, applicant pools, or a court in general are not used. Each result is checked a second time against these rules before it is shown, and that check can only lower a result, never raise it.

Registered lobbyists

For Miami-Dade County races, each candidate's givers are checked against the Miami-Dade Clerk of the Board's lobbyist registrations for the current year. A company or group counts as a lobbyist client when its name matches a client named in a registration, ignoring Inc, LLC, punctuation and capitalization. A person counts as a registered lobbyist when their first and last name match a registration and either the ZIP code on the registration matches the ZIP code on the gift or the occupation filed with the gift is lobbying, government affairs or consulting. Being registered means the person or company told the County it lobbies, or hires someone to lobby, County officials. It is not an accusation of anything. This check is only shown for County offices, because those are the officials County lobbyists lobby.

School Board races get the same check against the School Board's own list of registered lobbyists (fiscal years 2025-26 and 2026-27). That list gives no addresses. Where exactly one person with the same first and last name is registered with Miami-Dade County, the office address from that county registration is used; otherwise a person counts as a lobbyist only when the name matches and the occupation filed with the gift is lobbying, government affairs or consulting.

A company or firm is labeled Lobbyist's office when its filed address, including the suite number or PO box, is the registered office address of one or more registered lobbyists. That can be a lobbying or law firm, or a company that shares an office with people registered to lobby for its projects. The label states the shared address only.

Ballot measures

For each referendum and amendment, the site looks for registered committees on each side, their major funders, and organizations publicly supporting or opposing it in this election. A group's position on an earlier version of a measure is not shown as support or opposition this time.

Where the committees for and against a measure file electronically with the clerk, the money section is built from their own reports instead of web research: every contributor and its share of the total, the biggest places the money went, and the report dates covered. Each report's line items must add up to the totals the clerk posts for it, or the update is held back and the last matching version stays up.

Committees that file with a city clerk or the Miami-Dade Elections Department are read from VoterFocus, where each report's line items must match the clerk's posted totals. Committees that file with the state (constitutional amendments) are read from the Florida Division of Elections' contribution and expenditure records for 2025-2026, keeping only rows filed under the committee's exact name. When no committee is found for a question, the page says so and names the records that were checked and the date.

Each measure also shows a few numbers computed from those filings, with no words added: how much each side raised, from how many different givers, the largest giver's share, and what share came from businesses, people and political committees. "Per registered voter" is both sides' money added together, divided by the registered voters where the question is decided (the state's book-closing count for the Nov. 3, 2026 election; for the City of Miami, the county's registration totals for City of Miami districts 1-5 as of Oct. 1, 2026).

A giver is tied to what a question decides only with one of these labels, each linked to a source that says it outright: Party to the deal (the giver, or a company it co-owns, gets the contract, lease or sale the question decides); Current operator (the giver runs a business today on the property the question decides); At the property (the giver's filed address is the property the question decides); Political committee (the giver is itself a political committee, with where it files when it uses another name); Contractor (the giver, or the company its filing names it as leading, has had a contract with the government that put the question on the ballot, in effect in 2024 or later, shown in that government's own records). No other descriptions of givers' motives are written for these pages.

What the tool can get wrong

  • It can only find what has been published online. A candidate with little press coverage will have a thin profile.
  • Donor data for state and local races depends on news coverage and filings, and can lag the latest reports.
  • AI research can misread a source. That is why every claim links to where it came from. Please click through before relying on anything important.
  • This is not an official ballot and does not record votes. It is not affiliated with the Miami-Dade Supervisor of Elections or any government agency.

Order of endorsements

Newspaper editorial board endorsements are listed first, then every other endorsement in the order research found them. Editorial boards take sides too; they are listed first because voters often look for them, not because they count more.

Report an error

Every candidate and measure page has a Report a problem with this page link at the bottom. Reports go straight to the site owner, who reviews them within 24 hours and takes down anything false while it is corrected. You can also write to corrections@ballot305.org, open an issue on GitHub or message @realbritty on Instagram with the candidate's name and what you found. Corrections are made as fast as possible.

All of the code, including the exact research instructions and the fixed label list, is public on GitHub under the MIT license.
